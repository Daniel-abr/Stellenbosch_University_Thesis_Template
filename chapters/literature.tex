\mychapter{Literature}{Literature}{}
\label{chap:literature}

This chapter reviews the literature relevant to the design of a low-cost, portable potentiostat for \ac{DPV}. The purpose of this review is to establish the electrochemical and hardware fundamentals required to design such an instrument, and to survey the existing landscape of commercial and open-source potentiostat designs.

\mysection{Electrochemical Sensing}{Electrochemical Sensing}
\label{sec:electrochemical_background}

A potentiostat controls the potential between the reference and working electrode while simultaneously measuring the current flowing between the counter and the working electrode. This is a popular three-electrode setup for electrochemical measurement, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:3opamp}. The \ac{WE} is the focal area of electrochemical reactions \cite{lodh2023}. In a three-electrode system the potential around it is measured and manipulated to study the desired catalyst or analyte, where an analyte is a specific substance or chemical component that is the subject of analysis.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/3opamp.jpeg}
    \caption{The classic 3-operational-amplifier potentiostat topology, showing the control amplifier, RE buffer, and TIA connected to the three electrodes.}
    \label{fig:3opamp}
\end{figure}

The \ac{RE} provides a stable, known potential that is used as a benchmark to control and monitor the working electrode's potential \cite{cengiz2021}. Current flow through the reference electrode is minimal to ensure that its potential remains accurate \cite{cengiz2021}. The \ac{CE} (also called the auxiliary electrode) completes the circuit, allowing current to be sourced or sunk between it and the working electrode, which prevents the reference electrode from being affected \cite{cengiz2021}. The reaction at the counter electrode should be monitored, as corrosion at the electrode could interfere with measurement accuracy \cite{cui2023}. The three-electrode setup is the foundational hardware configuration for the voltammetric techniques discussed in Section~\ref{sec:voltammetry_family}, namely \ac{CV}, \ac{LSV}, \ac{SWV}, \ac{DPV}, and stripping voltammetry.

\mysubsection{Voltammetry as a Family of Techniques}{Voltammetry as a Family of Techniques}
\label{sec:voltammetry_family}

In any voltammetric measurement, applying a voltage to an electrode generates two types of current:

\begin{itemize}[label=\textbullet]
    \item \textbf{Faradaic current:} The actual current generated by the transfer of electrons during the oxidation or reduction of the target analyte \cite{alyamni2024}. This is the desired signal.
    \item \textbf{Charging (capacitive) current:} The background current generated simply by charging the electrical double-layer at the electrode surface, which acts like a capacitor caused by the Helmholtz plane \cite{alyamni2024}. This causes background noise. The double layer is modelled as the capacitance $C_{dl}$ in Section~\ref{sec:cell_model}.
\end{itemize}

The same three-electrode setup handles each of the following techniques in a different way:

\begin{itemize}[label=\textbullet]
    \item \textbf{Linear Sweep (\ac{LSV}) and Cyclic Voltammetry (\ac{CV}):} The potentiostat applies a continuous, linear voltage ramp (or a triangular sweep for CV). The counter electrode continuously sinks or sources the resulting Faradaic current.
    \item \textbf{Differential Pulse (\ac{DPV}) and Square Wave Voltammetry (\ac{SWV}):} Instead of a continuous ramp, the potentiostat applies a series of discrete voltage pulses or steps. By measuring the current at specific times during the pulse, the system can mathematically subtract the background capacitive (charging) current. This dramatically increases sensitivity, making DPV and SWV the preferred techniques for trace-level detection in biosensing applications.
\end{itemize}

\mysubsection{Equivalent Circuit of the Cell}{Equivalent Circuit of the Cell}
\label{sec:cell_model}

The two currents described in Section~\ref{sec:voltammetry_family} can be represented by an electrical model of the electrode--electrolyte interface. The widely used Randles equivalent circuit \cite{c5}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:randles_circuit}, models this interface as the solution resistance $R_s$ in series with the double-layer capacitance $C_{dl}$, which is in parallel with the charge-transfer resistance $R_{ct}$ and the Warburg impedance $Z_W$. The charging current flows into $C_{dl}$, while the Faradaic current flows through $R_{ct}$ and $Z_W$. $R_{ct}$ represents the resistance to electron transfer at the electrode surface, and $Z_W$ accounts for the diffusion of analyte towards the electrode at low frequencies \cite{bard2001, Lisdat2008}. The time constant $R_sC_{dl}$ sets how quickly the double layer charges after each potential step, which is central to how \ac{DPV} rejects the charging current.

\mysubsection{Differential Pulse Voltammetry}{Differential Pulse Voltammetry}
\label{sec:dpv_theory}
\label{sec:dpv_waveform}
\label{sec:dpv_sensitivity}
\label{sec:dpv_rejection}

\ac{DPV} was chosen as the focus of this project for its high sensitivity, ability to reject capacitive charging current and strong fit for the intended application \cite{Uslu2011}. Its excitation waveform is a staircase ramp with a small potential pulse superimposed on each step, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dpv_waveform}. The waveform is defined by the step potential, the pulse amplitude and the pulse width \cite{pineresearch_dpv}.

Current is not measured continuously. Two discrete samples are taken per step: $I_{\text{before}}$ immediately before the pulse is applied, and $I_{\text{end}}$ at the end of the pulse (labelled $i_1$ and $i_2$ in Figure~\ref{fig:dpv_waveform}). Their difference,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta I = I_{\text{end}} - I_{\text{before}},
    \label{eq:dpv_diff}
\end{equation}
is plotted against the step potential to form the voltammogram.

The sensitivity of \ac{DPV} comes from when these samples are taken. After a potential step $\Delta E$, the capacitive current decays exponentially \cite{palmsens_capacitive_current},
\begin{equation}
  I_c(t) = \frac{\Delta E}{R_s}\, e^{-t/R_sC_{dl}},
  \label{eq:ic}
\end{equation}
while the Faradaic current follows the Cottrell equation \cite{bard2001},
\begin{equation}
  I_f(t) = \frac{nFA\sqrt{D}\,C^*}{\sqrt{\pi t}},
  \label{eq:cottrell}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of electrons transferred, $F$ is Faraday's constant, $A$ is the electrode area, $D$ is the diffusion coefficient and $C^*$ is the bulk analyte concentration. Because the exponential decays far faster than $1/\sqrt{t}$ ~\cite[Sec.~1.2.4, Sec.~5.2.1]{bard2001}, sampling late in the step and late in the pulse allows the capacitive current to decay almost completely, so that $I_{c,\text{before}} \approx I_{c,\text{end}} \approx 0$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Delta I \approx I_{f,\text{end}} - I_{f,\text{before}}.
  \label{eq:delta_if}
\end{equation}
The full expansion is given in Appendix~\ref{app:dpv}. In practice this sets a minimum pulse width, since sampling at $t = 5R_sC_{dl}$ leaves less than \SI{1}{\percent} of the initial capacitive current ($e^{-5} \approx 0.007$). Because $\Delta I$ approximates the derivative of the Faradaic current--potential curve, the voltammogram forms a peak which, for a reversible system, lies half a pulse amplitude away from the half-wave potential $E_{1/2}$ \cite{bard2001}.

In continuous sweeps such as CV and LSV, by contrast, charging current is present throughout the measurement. The \ac{DPV} signal is therefore dominated by Faradaic current, which gives a much higher \ac{SNR} and lower limits of detection. The cost is reduced time resolution \cite{sciencedirect1993dpv}.

\mysubsection{Application to Antiviral Drug Residues in Water}{Application to Antiviral Drug Residues in Water}
\label{sec:dpv_applications}

South Africa runs the largest antiretroviral treatment programme in the world, and incomplete removal at wastewater treatment plants releases these drugs into surface water. Efavirenz in particular has been measured at concentrations ranging from ng/L to tens of \textmu g/L in surface water near treatment plant discharges \cite{vogt_arv_sa_waters}, yet South Africa has no monitoring programme or regulatory limit for these compounds \cite{schoeman2017efavirenz}. Existing measurements rely on laboratory chromatography--mass spectrometry (LCMS), which is costly and unsuited to field use.

Target analytes in these applications may be electroactive. These can include antiretrovirals like Efavirenz which undergoes irreversible oxidation and has been quantified by \ac{DPV} on disposable pencil graphite electrodes \cite{dogantopal2009efavirenz}. An example of antiviral medication is oseltamivir has been quantified by \ac{DPV} at modified paste microelectrodes \cite{pop2012oseltamivir} and shows a voltammetric response at gold electrodes \cite{avramov2011oseltamivir}. These studies used benchtop instruments and, in most cases, modified electrodes or adsorptive pre-concentration to reach low detection limits. A portable \ac{DPV} instrument is therefore a necessary step towards on-site screening, although the achievable detection limit ultimately depends on the electrode chemistry as well as the instrument.

\mysection{Potentiostat Fundamentals}{Potentiostat Fundamentals}
\label{sec:potentiostat_fundamentals}

This section explores the fundamentals of a potentiostat, namely its role, design, performance parameters, and implementation approaches.

\mysubsection{The Role of a Potentiostat}{The Role of a Potentiostat}
\label{sec:role_potentiostat}

The role of a potentiostat is to manage the control amplifier and the TIA, and to buffer the reference electrode. For reference electrode buffering, the electrode must remain perfectly stable at a known half-cell potential. To prevent the voltage from drifting, zero current is allowed to flow through the electrode. This is achieved using an electrometer, an operational amplifier with an extremely high input impedance \cite{iest2026threeelectrode}.

The control amplifier drives the system by constantly comparing the desired input voltage, set by a DAC, with the actual voltage measured at the reference electrode. The resulting error signal is minimised by forcing current through the counter electrode and into the solution, which actively compensates for any changes in the electrochemical cell's resistance. The TIA holds the working electrode at a 0\,V reference while catching every electron produced or consumed by the reaction \cite{bard2001}. These electrons generate a small Faradaic current that is converted into a potential, which can then be measured by an \ac{ADC} \cite{bard2001}. The precision of the measurements made by the TIA depends not only on the AFEs noise performance but when the sample is taken. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dpv_sensitivity}, the capacitive and Faradaic currents are distinguished by their differing decay times. Therefore, to sample accurately, precision timing and specific, predictable sampling windows are required. The occurrence of a timing jitter would directly degrade the signal--to-noise ratio regardless of the AFE's noise performance. To ensure accurate measurements key performance parameters need ot be considered when building and designing a potentiostat.

\mysubsection{Key Performance Parameters}{Key Performance Parameters}
\label{sec:key_performance_parameters}

This section establishes the criteria used to evaluate the effectiveness of the hardware, namely current measurement range and resolution, compliance voltage, noise floor, bandwidth, and technique support.

\begin{itemize}[label=\textbullet]
    \item \textbf{Current measurement range and resolution:} In drug residue sensing, very small Faradaic signals are generated. The potentiostat must be able to accurately measure down to the nanoampere (nA) or picoampere (pA) range without clipping or saturating the signal.

    \item \textbf{Compliance voltage:} The control amplifier must drive the cell resistance $R_s + R_{ct}$ of Section~\ref{sec:cell_model}. If this resistance is high, the control amplifier needs a high compliance voltage to push the necessary current through the counter electrode \cite{pineresearch_compliance}.

    \item \textbf{Noise floor and \ac{LOD}:} The LOD  is constrained by the noise floor of the potentiostat's AFE. In high-sensitivity applications, the TIA requires large feedback resistors to convert picoampere-level Faradaic currents into measurable voltages. However, these resistive components inherently generate thermal noise. Therefore, a device targeting nanoampere or picoampere signals requires a potentiostat topology with an exceptionally low electrical noise floor \cite{graeme1996photodiode,adi_mt059}.

    \item \textbf{Bandwidth / scan rate limits:} This is the maximum speed at which the potentiostat can alter the applied potential. Fast techniques such as \ac{SWV} require high-bandwidth operational amplifiers to avoid signal distortion \cite{bard2001}.

    \item \textbf{Technique support:} A system's ability to run CV, DPV, or \ac{EIS} depends on its \ac{DAC} resolution and internal waveform generators \cite{cruz2020}.
\end{itemize}

\mysubsection{Commercial Instruments}{Commercial Instruments}
\label{sec:commercial_instruments}

Commercial systems such as the PalmSens4 \cite{PalmSens4}, EmStat \cite{MultiEmStat4} and Metrohm Autolab \cite{MetrohmAutolab} are the benchmark for electrochemical analysis, offering wide dynamic ranges (pA to mA), factory calibration and mature software. However, they are expensive, bulky and closed-source, which prevents their integration into low-cost portable systems. Their value lies in providing the performance reference against which open-source and custom hardware is validated \cite{palmsens2023}. Open-source designs have therefore largely solved the cost and accessibility problems of commercial instruments, but not yet the combination of high sensitivity and fully standalone operation that field-testing requires

\mysubsection{Discrete Open-Source Designs}{Discrete Open-Source Designs}
\label{sec:early_opensource}
\label{sec:general_purpose_opensource}
\label{sec:portable_wireless}

CheapStat \cite{rowe2011} showed that basic analytical electrochemistry was achievable with a \ac{BOM} under \$100. DStat \cite{dryden2015} closed much of the performance gap, reaching near-commercial sensitivity with a discrete analog front-end and supporting CV, SWV, DPV and CA through a Python \ac{GUI}. However, it depends on a USB-connected PC for power and control, which rules out field use. Smartphone-integrated designs such as the UWED \cite{ainla2018} achieved portability by moving processing and display to a phone over \ac{BLE}, but this ties the hardware to a companion app that must be maintained across operating system updates.

\mysubsection{AFE-Based and Web-Hosted Designs}{AFE-Based and Web-Hosted Designs}
\label{sec:eis_afe_designs}
\label{sec:selfhosted_webinterface}

Integrated mixed-signal \acp{AFE} have largely replaced discrete op-amp circuits in recent designs. Systems such as ABE-Stat \cite{jenkins2019}, HunStat \cite{hunstat2024} and HunStat2 \cite{hunstat2_2025} characterise this generation: using \acp{SoC} such as the AD5941, they support techniques up to \ac{EIS} with sub-milliwatt power consumption, low noise and a small footprint \cite{hunstat2024,hunstat2_2025}. However, configuring the \ac{AFE} requires complex register-level firmware, and these devices typically operate as data-loggers with no user interface, relying on raw serial output for analysis \cite{hunstat2_2025}. To remove the dependence on a companion app, ESP32-based designs such as NanoStat \cite{lee2022}, DM-Stat \cite{dmstat2024} and SaxStat \cite{saxstat2025} host an embedded web server. Any browser on any platform can then control the instrument without installed software, at the cost of \ac{MCU} memory \cite{lee2022}.

These web-hosted designs are nevertheless headless and cannot be operated without a second device. Early designs such as CheapStat included a basic display, but none of the surveyed systems combines an integrated \ac{AFE}, a browser interface and a standalone onboard display. Such a dual-interface instrument would allow quick standalone measurements in the field while retaining web-based data access, and this is the gap addressed by this project.
