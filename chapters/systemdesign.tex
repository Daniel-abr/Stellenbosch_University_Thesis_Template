\section{System Architecture}
The proposed hardware architecture of the potentiostat illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:system_architecture} is divided into high-level software logic and discrete hardware components. The architecture is designed to be modular, allowing for easy integration of various components. The analog front-end serves as the core of the system, providing the necessary tools for electrochemical measurements. The microcontroller manages data acquisition, processing, and communication. The physical user interface allows for local control and user input and control. The three-electrode setup ensures accurate electrochemical measurements, making the system suitable for detecting trace levels of antiretroviral medications in water samples as discussed in Section \ref{sec:3opamp_topology}.

The software architecture manages the analytical core and connectivity of the system. It is driven by real-time operating system and features an embedded web server and Wi-Fi capabilities for data extraction and review alongside data storage. The analytical core manages the DPV waveform generation and differential current calculations, while a local GUI and display driver ensure the physical interface is operational without the need for an external smart device. This is especially useful in field deployments where a smartphone or computer may not be available, such as remote water testing sites, under-resourced communities.

It is possible to model the electrochemical cell as a Randle's equivalent circuit, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:randles_circuit}. After defining the system architecture required to interface with the electrochemical cell, it is required to mathematically model what the cell presents to the system. The Randle equivalent circuit is a complex non-ideal impedance model that changes depending on frequency, applied potential and the electrochemical reaction occurring at the working electrode. To design a control loop capable of driving this load reliably, the cell must first be reduced to an equivalent electrical model. A widely used representation of the cell is the Randles equivalent circuit \cite{Laschuk2021}. This equivalent circuit models the electrode and electrolyte interface as a series solution resistance ($R_s$) in series with a parallel combination of the double-layer capacitance ($C_{dl}$) and the charge-transfer resistance ($R_{ct}$). The Warburg impedance ($Z_W$) is included to account for diffusion effects at low frequencies. This model allows for the analysis of the electrochemical cell's behavior under different conditions and is essential for designing the potentiostat's control loop \cite{sannino2025potentiostat}.

The link between DPV waveforms and the Randles equivalent circuit is found through identifying the electrical demand it places on the potentiostat. The combination of the $R_s$ and $R_{ct}$ resistors is the total resistive load that must be driven by the control amplifier to sustain the target faradaic current. This resistive load dictates the compliance voltage that the system requires \cite{pineresearch_compliance}. The combination of $R_s$ and $C_{dl}$ form a RC time constant which dictates how quickly the double layer capacitance charges after each step in potential. This time constant governs how long a DPV pulse should be held to ensure that the background current has sufficiently decayed relative to the faradaic current \cite{bard2001}. Rather than being arbitrary selections, parameters such as pulse width, TIA gain, and control-amplifier bandwidth are strictly dictated by the expected $R_s$, $C_{dl}$, and $R_{ct}$ values of the target analyte-electrode interface. If the sampling scheme or amplifier bandwidth fails to match this characteristic RC time constant, the system cannot effectively filter out the unwanted capacitive component of the measured current\cite{sannino2025potentiostat}.

\subsection{Proving Capacitive Current Rejection in DPV}

DPV's effectiveness relies on the different decay rates of the two current components generated at an electrode following a potential step.

\subsubsection{Total Current at the Electrode}

When a potential step is applied, the total measured current is the sum of the faradaic and capacitive components:

\begin{equation}
I_{total}(t) = I_f(t) + I_c(t)
\end{equation}

\centering

\midrule
Capacitive ($I_c$) & \makecell[l]{Charging the electrical \\ double layer} & $e^{-t/RC}$ & \makecell[l]{Very fast \\ (exponential)} \\
\addlinespace
Faradaic ($I_f$) & \makecell[l]{Electron transfer} & $1/\sqrt{t}$ & \makecell[l]{Slower \\ (inverse square root)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\label{tab:current_types}

The capacitive current models the electrode-solution interface as an $RC$ circuit, decaying exponentially \cite{palmsens_capacitive_current}:

\begin{equation}
I_c(t) = \frac{\Delta E}{R_s} \, e^{-t/R_sC_{dl}}
\end{equation}

Where: 
\begin{itemize}[label=\textbullet]

\item $R_s$ is the solution resistance.
\item $C_{dl}$ is the double-layer capacitance.
\item $\Delta E$ is the applied potential step.

\end{itemize}

The faradaic current is governed by the Cottrell equation for planar diffusion, decaying with the inverse square root of time \cite{bard2001}:

\begin{equation}
I_f(t) = \frac{nFA\sqrt{D}\,C^*}{\sqrt{\pi t}}
\end{equation}

Where: 

\begin{itemize}[label=\textbullet]
    \item $n$ is the number of electrons transferred.
    \item $F$ is Faraday's constant.
    \item $A$ is the electrode area.
    \item $D$ is the diffusion coefficient.
    \item $C^*$ is the bulk analyte concentration.
\end{itemize}

Because an exponential function ($e^{-t/RC}$) tends toward zero faster than an inverse square root function ($1/\sqrt{t}$), by waiting for a sufficient amount of time after applying the pulse ensures the capacitive current disappeared while the faradaic current remains. 
\subsubsection{The Pulse-Sampling Scheme}

To utilise the property difference between the two current types, DPV applies a series of short voltage pulses superimposed on a linear staircase ramp. To isolate the faradaic current, the potentiostat samples the current twice per pulse cycle:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $I_{before}$: sampled just before the pulse is applied.
    \item $I_{end}$: sampled at the very end of the pulse duration ($t = \tau_{pulse}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Differential Proof}

The final output plotted in a DPV voltammogram is the difference between these two samples:

\begin{equation}
\Delta I = I_{end} - I_{before}
\end{equation}

Expanding using the component currents:

\begin{equation}
\Delta I = \left(I_{f,end} + I_{c,end}\right) - \left(I_{f,before} + I_{c,before}\right)
\end{equation}

Finding a solution to the decay mathematics to solve the problem:

\begin{itemize}[label=\textbullet]
    \item Since $I_{before}$ is sampled just before the pulse, the system has been at a baseline potential for a sufficient amount of time so that any previous capacitive transient current has decayed completely. Thus, $I_{c,before} \approx 0$.
    \item Since $I_{end}$ is sampled at the very end of the pulse duration, the new capacitive transient caused by the pulse has also decayed to near zero. Thus, $I_{c,end} \approx 0$.

\end{itemize}

Therefore, the differential in Equation 3.5 simplifies to:

\begin{equation}
\Delta I \approx I_{f,end} - I_{f,before}
\end{equation}

By subtracting $I_{before}$ from $I_{end}$, DPV mathematically rejects the background charging current, leaving only the faradaic signal of interest.

\subsubsection{Why the Output Forms a Peak}

Because $\Delta I$ represents the change in faradaic current for a small, discrete step in potential ($\Delta E$), the resulting plot of $\Delta I$ versus $E$ is effectively an approximation of the derivative of a standard current-potential curve:

\begin{equation}
\Delta I \approx \frac{dI_f}{dE} \cdot \Delta E
\end{equation}

For a \glslink{electrochemically_reversible_system}{reversible system} governed by the Nernst equation, taking the derivative of the standard sigmoidal voltammogram produces a Gaussian-like peak. This peak reaches its maximum amplitude exactly at the half-wave potential ($E_{1/2}$), providing an incredibly sensitive and easily quantifiable signal \cite{Laborda2014}.

\subsection{The Closed-Loop Transfer Function of a Negative-Feedback Loop}
\label{sec:closed-loop-tf}
 
The stability of the control amplifier is dictated by a closed negative feedback loop \cite{bard2001}. The control amplifier continuously compares the input command voltage at its inverting terminal with the feedback voltage measured at the reference electrode \cite{bard2001}. The output voltage at the counter electrode is constantly adjusted to force the required current to ensure that $E_{\text{wk vs.\ ref}} = \sum E_{\text{in}}$. Consider the system above where $A(s)$ is the control amplifier and $B(s)$ is the connection between the reference electrode and the counter electrode of the electrochemical cell. By analysing this system using classical control theory, the following transfer function was found \cite{c2}:
 
\begin{equation}
T(s) \equiv \frac{e_o(s)}{E_{\text{in}}(s)} = \frac{A(s)}{1+A(s)\,\beta(s)}
\label{eq:closed_loop_tf}
\end{equation}
 
The open loop gain is defined as
 
\begin{equation}
L(s) \equiv A(s)\,\beta(s)
\label{eq:loop_gain_def}
\end{equation}
 
The poles of the closed-loop system $T(s)$, which govern the behaviour of the transient response, are the roots of the characteristic equation \cite{c2}:
 
\begin{equation}
1+L(s) = 0
\label{eq:char_eq}
\end{equation}
 
The Bode phase margin stability criterion can be used to determine the stability of the system. For a negative-feedback loop with loop gain $L(j\omega)$, the closed loop is stable if, at the frequency $\omega_{cl}$ where $|L(j\omega_{cl})| = 1$ (0~dB), the total phase shift is still greater than $-180^\circ$. The margin between the actual phase and $-180^\circ$ at that frequency is the \emph{phase margin},
 
\begin{equation}
\varphi_{PM} = 180^\circ - |\angle L(j\omega_{cl})|
\label{eq:phase_margin_def}
\end{equation}
 
A phase margin $\leq 0^\circ$ means the closed-loop poles lie in the right half of the $s$-plane and the system oscillates \cite{c2}.
 
\subsection{Application to the Control Loop}
\label{sec:control-loop}
 
The control amplifier, the electrochemical cell, and the reference-electrode buffer form a negative-feedback loop that maps directly onto the structure of Section~\ref{sec:closed-loop-tf}. Note that the output $e_o(s)$ drives the counter electrode (CE), and the feedback signal returned to the summing junction is the potential $E_{\text{ref}}(s)$ sensed at the RE, so $\beta(s) = E_{\text{ref}}(s)/e_o(s)$. Bard and Faulkner \cite{bard2001} discuss the characteristics of an open-loop gain with a single dominant pole; therefore, this model is adopted for both the control amplifier and the TIA.
 
The control amplifier's open-loop gain near crossover is
 
\begin{equation}
A_{\text{ctrl}}(s) = \frac{\omega_{t,\text{ctrl}}}{s}
\label{eq:actrl}
\end{equation}
 
with $\omega_{t,\text{ctrl}} = 2\pi \cdot \mathrm{GBW}_{\text{ctrl}}$ (read from the chosen amplifier's datasheet).
 
By only looking at the simplified Randles circuit discussed above, $\Ru$ is in series with the double-layer capacitance $\Cdl$ \cite{c5,bard2001,c7,c8}. For small-signal stability analysis, the standard Randles circuit is simplified to a high-frequency-reduced model consisting only of the uncompensated resistance ($\Ru$) in series with the double-layer capacitance ($\Cdl$). The charge-transfer resistance ($R_{ct}$) and Warburg impedance ($Z_W$) are omitted, as they govern the low-frequency Faradaic response targeted by techniques such as CV and DPV, rather than the high-frequency transient stability.

From the perspective of the control amplifier's output, $\Ru$ and $\Cdl$ therefore form a series impedance path from $e_o(s)$ to virtual ground, and the RE senses the potential at the node \emph{between} them. $B(s)$ is therefore the transfer function of the voltage divider; let $I(s)$ be the current flowing through the series path:
 
\begin{equation}
I(s) = \frac{e_o(s)}{\Ru + \dfrac{1}{s\Cdl}}
\label{eq:current_divider}
\end{equation}
 
The potential sensed at the RE node, relative to the virtual-ground WE, is the voltage dropped across the capacitive branch:
 
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{ref}}(s) = I(s) \cdot \frac{1}{s\Cdl}
\label{eq:eref}
\end{equation}
 
Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:current_divider} and \eqref{eq:eref} into each other, it is found that
 
\begin{equation}
\beta(s) \equiv \frac{E_{\text{ref}}(s)}{e_o(s)} = \frac{1}{1+s\Ru\Cdl} = \frac{1}{1+s\,\tau_{\text{cell}}}, \qquad \tau_{\text{cell}} \equiv \Ru\Cdl
\label{eq:beta_cell}
\end{equation}
 
This single-pole form for the cell's feedback factor, derived here as an $\Ru$--$\Cdl$ divider, is the same reduced cell model used to analyse control-loop bandwidth and IR-compensation limits in the classical potentiostat-instrumentation literature \cite{c5,c7,c8}.
 
To find the loop gain, Equations~\eqref{eq:actrl} and \eqref{eq:beta_cell} can be combined to find that
 
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{ctrl}}(s) = \frac{\omega_{t,\text{ctrl}}}{s\,(1+s\,\tau_{\text{cell}})}
\label{eq:lctrl}
\end{equation}
 
This shows that there is an integrator pole at the origin from the control amplifier combined with a real pole at $s = -1/\tau_{\text{cell}}$ (from $\beta(s)$). Setting $|L_{\text{ctrl}}(j\omega)| = 1$ and solving for the high-bandwidth case $\omega \gg 1/\tau_{\text{cell}}$ gives the crossover frequency
 
\begin{equation}
\omega_{cl,\text{ctrl}} = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{t,\text{ctrl}}}{\tau_{\text{cell}}}}
\label{eq:wcl_ctrl}
\end{equation}
 
At this frequency the integrator pole contributes a fixed $-90^\circ$ of phase, and the cell pole contributes additional lag approaching $-90^\circ$ as $\omega_{cl,\text{ctrl}}\,\tau_{\text{cell}}$ grows, so by the criterion of Equation~\eqref{eq:phase_margin_def} the phase margin
 
\begin{equation}
\varphi_{PM,\text{ctrl}} = 180^\circ - |\angle L_{\text{ctrl}}(j\omega_{cl,\text{ctrl}})|
\label{eq:pm_ctrl}
\end{equation}
 
degrades as $\tau_{\text{cell}} = \Ru\Cdl$ increases.
 
\subsection{Reference Electrode Buffer}
\label{sec:re-buffer}
 
The third amplifier is the RE buffer, which is connected as a unity-gain voltage follower where its output is wired back to its inverting input. The inverting input draws no current from the RE, so $B(s) = 1$ since it is just a direct feedback path; therefore:
 
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{buf}}(s) = \frac{A_{\text{buf}}(s)}{1+A_{\text{buf}}(s)}
\label{eq:tbuf}
\end{equation}
 
For $|A_{\text{buf}}(s)| \gg 1$ over the frequency range of interest, the transfer function reduces to $T_{\text{buf}}(s) \approx 1$, thus no pole is contributed \cite{c3}.
 
\subsection{Practical Stability Margin and Limits of This Model}
\label{sec:practical-limits}
 
Equation~\eqref{eq:wcl_ctrl} shows that $\omega_{cl,\text{ctrl}}$ scales as $1/\sqrt{\tau_{\text{cell}}}$, so the control loop's margin depends on how large $\tau_{\text{cell}} = \Ru\Cdl$ is relative to the amplifier's own response time. $\Ru$ is set by the electrolyte and cell geometry and is typically tens to a few hundred ohms for a well-conducting aqueous electrolyte \cite{bard2001}. Because both loops share the same $\Cdl$ but the control loop's time constant is scaled by $\Ru$ rather than $\Rf$, $\tau_{\text{cell}} \ll \Rf\Cdl$, and the control loop's pole sits proportionally further from its own crossover frequency than the TIA loop's pole does from its crossover.
 
\subsection{Breaking Down the TIA Stability Proof}
\label{sec:tia-stability}
 
The TIAs are used for sensitive electrochemical techniques and are highly susceptible to the double-layer capacitance of the chemical cell. This proof demonstrates that the electrochemical cell isn't just a passive component that is measured --- it becomes an active component of the amplifier's control loop.
 
\subsubsection{The Photodiode Analogy}
\label{sec:photodiode-analogy}
 
For decades, electrical engineers have used transimpedance amplifiers (TIAs) to read the tiny currents generated by photodiodes. A photodiode naturally has a small amount of parasitic junction capacitance. This capacitance notoriously causes the TIA to become unstable and oscillate, so engineers developed a standard mathematical formula to fix it by adding a specific compensation capacitor \cite{graeme1996photodiode}. In a potentiostat the working electrode connects directly to the inverting input of the TIA; the chemical interface between the electrode and the solution has a double-layer capacitance ($\Cdl$). The $\Cdl$ acts as a capacitor shunting the inverting input to ground. The compensation capacitor is implemented in the proof below to maintain stability \cite{graeme1996photodiode}.
 
\subsubsection{Deriving the Transfer Function}

The faradaic current from the cell ($I_{\text{in}}$) must go somewhere. Assuming an ideal op-amp, it splits into two paths: through the feedback resistor ($\Rf$) and through the double-layer capacitance ($\Cdl$). In the Laplace domain, that current equation is:
 
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{in}} = V_n\,s\Cdl + \frac{V_n - V_{\text{out}}}{\Rf}
\label{eq:kcl}
\end{equation}
 
In a real-world application an op-amp has a finite open-loop gain $A(s)$, so the voltage at the inverting node ($V_n$) isn't perfectly zero. It is governed by
 
\begin{equation}
V_n = -\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{A(s)}
\label{eq:vn}
\end{equation}
 
By substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:vn} into Equation~\eqref{eq:kcl}, the following equation is found:
 
\begin{equation}
Z_T(s) \equiv \frac{V_{\text{out}}}{I_{\text{in}}} = -\frac{\Rf}{1+\dfrac{1+s\Rf\Cdl}{A(s)}}
\label{eq:zt}
\end{equation}
 
\subsubsection{Loop Gain and Phase Margin Collapse}
\label{sec:tia-loop-gain}
 
The stability of the TIA is fundamentally dictated by the interaction between the open-loop gain of the operational amplifier, $A(s)$, and the noise gain, $NG(s)$, of the feedback network. In this topology, the double-layer capacitance of the electrochemical cell, $\Cdl$, acts as a shunt capacitance at the inverting node. Consequently, the noise gain is defined as:
 
\begin{equation}
NG(s) = 1+s\Rf\Cdl
\label{eq:ng}
\end{equation}
 
This formulation demonstrates that $\Cdl$ introduces a zero into the feedback network at
 
\begin{equation}
f_z = \frac{1}{2\pi \Rf\Cdl}
\label{eq:fz}
\end{equation}
 
In classical control theory, the loop gain, $T(s)$, is evaluated as the ratio of the open-loop gain to the noise gain \cite{graeme1996photodiode}. Assuming a dominant-pole model for the operational amplifier where $A(s) = \omega_t/s$ (where $\omega_t$ is the gain-bandwidth product in rad/s), the loop gain becomes:
 
\begin{equation}
T(s) = \frac{\omega_t/s}{1+s\Rf\Cdl}
\label{eq:tia_loop_gain}
\end{equation}
 
This equation reveals that the zero introduced by $\Cdl$ in the noise gain creates a second pole in the loop gain at
 
\begin{equation}
\omega_{p2} = \frac{1}{\Rf\Cdl}
\label{eq:wp2}
\end{equation}
 
adding to the op-amp's pole at the origin \cite{franco2014design}.
 
By setting $|T(j\omega)| = 1$, the crossover frequency for high-bandwidth scenarios ($\omega \gg \omega_{p2}$) is approximated as:
 
\begin{equation}
\omega_{cl} = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_t}{\Rf\Cdl}}
\label{eq:wcl_tia}
\end{equation}
 
At this crossover frequency, both poles contribute approximately $-90^\circ$ of phase shift. As the total phase shift approaches $-180^\circ$, the phase margin collapses toward $0^\circ$. In practical high-sensitivity potentiostat applications, where $\Rf$ must be sufficiently large to resolve nanoampere-to-picoampere currents, and $\Cdl$ can vary significantly based on electrode geometry, this degraded phase margin results in severe ringing, overshoot, or sustained oscillation \cite{adi_mt059}.
 
\subsubsection{System Stabilization via Capacitive Feedback Compensation}
\label{sec:cf-compensation}
 
To restore closed-loop stability, the phase margin must be increased by mitigating the effect of the $\Cdl$-induced second pole. This is achieved through passive compensation by placing a small feedback capacitor, $C_f$, in parallel with the transimpedance feedback resistor, $\Rf$.
 
The addition of $C_f$ introduces a compensating pole into the noise gain profile, strategically positioned prior to the zero caused by $\Cdl$. To achieve an optimally flat (Butterworth) closed-loop response with a phase margin between $45^\circ$ and $65^\circ$, the compensating pole is placed at the geometric mean of the noise gain zero and the amplifier's gain-bandwidth product (GBW) \cite{graeme1996photodiode}. The required compensation capacitance is calculated as:
 
\begin{equation}
C_f = \sqrt{\frac{\Cdl}{2\pi \Rf \cdot \mathrm{GBW}}}
\label{eq:cf}
\end{equation}
 
This standard stabilisation technique demonstrates that the physical characteristics of the electrochemical cell actively dictate the stability parameters of the analog front-end. Consequently, the size of $C_f$ must be correctly chosen according to the maximum expected $\Cdl$ of the cell, rather than operating as a fixed, independent parameter of the amplifier circuit.
