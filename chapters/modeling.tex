\chapter{System Modelling and Design}
\label{chap:modeling}

This chapter models the measurement path of the OpenStat and uses the model to choose between the high-speed TIA (HSTIA) and the low-power TIA (LPTIA) of the AD5941. The conditions under which the chosen amplifier remains stable with an electrode connected are also discussed.


\section{Signal Chain}
\label{sec:signal_chain}

Figure~\ref{fig:signal_chain} shows the measurement path of the OpenStat.
The low-power DAC (LPDAC) of the AD5941 sets $V_\mathrm{BIAS}$, and the potentiostat amplifier drives the CE until the RE reaches the same
potential. The HSTIA holds the WE at $V_\mathrm{ZERO}$ and
converts the cell current to a voltage, which is amplified, digitised and
filtered before the ESP32-S3 averages it into $I_\mathrm{before}$ and
$I_\mathrm{end}$ (Section~\ref{sec:sw_sampling}).

In the feedback loop two aspects need to be considered, the fixed internal load resistor $R_L \approx \SI{110}{\ohm}$ \cite[pp.~10, 50]{ad5941_ds}, and the capacitor $C_f$ across the gain resistor $R_\mathrm{TIA}$, which is programmable from \SIrange{1}{63}{\pico\farad} \cite[p.~52]{ad5941_ds}. 

\section{Choice and Operating Limits of the TIA}
\label{sec:tia_choice}

The AD5941 has two TIAs that can measure the WE current. The LPTIA is
intended for continuous, low-power sensing and offers gain resistors up to \SI{512}{\kilo\ohm} \cite[p.~10]{ad5941_ds},
which allows smaller currents to be amplified. 

\subsection{High-Speed or Low-Power TIA}
\label{sec:hstia_lptia}

According to the datasheet, the recommended configuration for voltammetry is to use the HSTIA. This drives the CE from the LPDAC through the potentiostat loop,
disconnects the output capacitors so that the potential can
step quickly, and measures the WE current on the HSTIA
\cite[Table~21, p.~38]{ad5941_ds}. The OpenStat follows this configuration.

The LPTIA was not ruled out on speed; with a small feedback capacitor it can
also follow a \SI{50}{\milli\second} pulse, and its larger gain resistors would give about three times finer current resolution (\SI{512}{\kilo\ohm} against \SI{160}{\kilo\ohm}). The HSTIA was nevertheless preferred for practical reasons. Its
feedback capacitor $C_f$ is set in firmware \cite[p.~52]{ad5941_ds}, whereas
the LPTIA uses an external capacitor fixed at assembly, so the compensation
can be changed without modifying the board. Its bandwidth also covers
impedance measurement up to \SI{200}{\kilo\hertz}, against
\SI{300}{\hertz} for the low-bandwidth loop \cite[p.~9]{ad5941_ds}, which
leaves impedance spectroscopy open as future work. On its most sensitive range the HSTIA resolves \SI{0.23}{\nano\ampere} per ADC code (Table~\ref{tab:sw_ranges}), and averaging 18 samples improves this further (Section~\ref{sec:sw_sampling}). This meets Objective~2, but the LPTIA would be the better choice if the device were extended to sub-\textmu g/L concentrations without preconcentration.

\subsection{Stability with a Cell Connected}
\label{sec:stability}

The AD5941 applies a voltage to the connected cell through feedback. The potentiostat amplifier drives the CE until the RE equals $V_\mathrm{BIAS}$, the HSTIA holds the WE at $V_\mathrm{ZERO}$, and the
current is whatever the cell draws at that potential, up to
\SI{750}{\micro\ampere} \cite[p.~9]{ad5941_ds}. The cell's electrical properties affect how both amplifiers behave. In the control loop, $R_u$ and $C_{dl}$ add a pole at
$1/(2\pi R_uC_{dl})$. The gain-bandwidth product of this amplifier is fixed in
the AD5941, so this loop cannot be compensated and instead limits the cells
that can be driven \cite{bard2001}. 

The TIA loop can be shaped through $C_f$. Seen from the inverting input of the HSTIA, the cell is $C_{dl}$ in series
with $R_\Sigma = R_u + R_L$ to the solution, which the control loop holds at
a fixed potential. Without $C_f$ the noise gain is
\begin{equation}
  NG(s) = 1 + \frac{R_\mathrm{TIA}}{R_\Sigma + 1/sC_{dl}}
        = \frac{1 + s\left(R_\mathrm{TIA} + R_\Sigma\right)C_{dl}}{1 + sR_\Sigma C_{dl}},
  \label{eq:ng_series}
\end{equation}
with a zero at $f_z = 1/2\pi(R_\mathrm{TIA}+R_\Sigma)C_{dl}$ and a pole at
$f_p = 1/2\pi R_\Sigma C_{dl}$, above which it levels off at
$1 + R_\mathrm{TIA}/R_\Sigma$. Unlike the photodiode amplifier often used as a
model for this circuit \cite{graeme1996photodiode}, whose noise gain keeps
rising, $R_\Sigma$ isolates $C_{dl}$ at high frequency. The loop crosses over
where the open-loop gain of the amplifier, $\mathrm{GBW}/f$, has fallen to the
noise gain. With no cell connected the noise gain is 1 and the crossover is at
the full GBW; with the electrode connected the crossover moves down to the
plateau,
$f_c \approx \mathrm{GBW}\cdot R_\Sigma/(R_\mathrm{TIA}+R_\Sigma)$. For a
single-pole amplifier with $f_c \gg f_z$, the phase margin is
approximately \cite{c2}
\begin{equation}
  \phi_\mathrm{PM} \approx \arctan\left(\frac{f_c}{f_p}\right),
  \label{eq:pm_approx}
\end{equation}
so a margin of at least \SI{45}{\degree} requires $f_c \geq f_p$, or
\begin{equation}
  R_\Sigma \geq \sqrt{\frac{R_\mathrm{TIA}}{2\pi\,\mathrm{GBW}\,C_{dl}}}.
  \label{eq:rmin}
\end{equation}
The margin is smallest on the largest gain resistor with the smallest series
resistance. The datasheet does not give the GBW of the HSTIA, so a
conservative \SI{1}{\mega\hertz} is assumed; a larger value would only increase the
margins. Table~\ref{tab:pm} gives the margin on the \SI{160}{\kilo\ohm} range, computed from the full loop gain. The full derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:tia}.

With $C_f = \SI{31}{\pico\farad}$ on every range, the C220AT ($C_{dl} \approx \SIrange{1.3}{5}{\micro\farad}$, estimated from a typical \SIrange{10}{40}{\micro\farad\per\centi\metre\squared} \cite[Sec.~1.2]{bard2001} over its \SI{0.126}{\centi\metre\squared} working electrode) has a margin of at least \SI{44}{\degree} with no solution resistance and \SI{70}{\degree} with $R_u = \SI{100}{\ohm}$, and the dummy cell WE~A of Section~\ref{sec:randles_test} has \SI{53}{\degree}, consistent with its stable readings. Margins below \SI{20}{\degree} arise only near \SI{100}{\nano\farad} on the sensitive ranges, where the TIA rings briefly after each step. With $\zeta \approx \phi_\mathrm{PM}/100$ and $t_s \approx 4.6/(\zeta \cdot 2\pi f_c)$ \cite{c2}, the worst case settles to within \SI{1}{\percent} in about \SIrange{1}{2}{\milli\second}, long before the averaging window opens. Raising $C_f$ to \SI{63}{\pico\farad} would improve these margins with only a firmware change and is recommended as future work (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).
