The AD5941 analog front-end forms the analog
core of the system. It applies the potential to the three-electrode cell and measures the resulting current. The ESP32-S3 controls the AD5941 over SPI, generates the
DPV waveform timing, computes the differential current and stores the
results. The main while loop manages these tasks alongside an embedded web server, which
serves the results over Wi-Fi, and a local display driver, which allows the
device to be operated through its buttons and screen without an external
device.

To design the loops that drive and measure the cell, the cell is reduced to
an electrical model. The widely used Randles equivalent circuit
\cite{c5}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:randles_circuit}, models the
electrode--electrolyte interface as the solution resistance $R_s$ in series
with the double-layer capacitance $C_{dl}$, which is in parallel with the
charge-transfer resistance $R_{ct}$ and the Warburg impedance $Z_W$. $Z_W$
accounts for diffusion at low frequencies \cite{bard2001, Lisdat2008}.


Each element places a specific demand on the potentiostat. The total
resistance $R_s + R_{ct}$ is the load that the control amplifier must drive
to sustain the faradaic current, and so sets the required compliance voltage
\cite{pineresearch_compliance}. The time constant $R_sC_{dl}$ sets how
quickly the double layer charges after each potential step, and therefore
how long a DPV pulse must be held before the capacitive current has decayed
\cite{bard2001}. $C_{dl}$ also enters the stability of both amplifier loops
(Section~\ref{sec:afe_stability}). The pulse width, TIA gain and amplifier
bandwidth are therefore set by the expected $R_s$, $C_{dl}$ and $R_{ct}$ of
the electrode--analyte interface rather than chosen arbitrarily.

\subsection{Capacitive Current Rejection in DPV}
\label{sec:dpv_rejection}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:dpv_waveform}, DPV samples the current just
before each pulse ($I_\text{before}$) and at the end of the pulse
($I_\text{end}$), and reports $\Delta I = I_\text{end} - I_\text{before}$
(Equation~\ref{eq:dpv_diff}). After a potential step $\Delta E$, the
capacitive current decays exponentially
\cite{palmsens_capacitive_current},
\begin{equation}
  I_c(t) = \frac{\Delta E}{R_s}\, e^{-t/R_sC_{dl}},
  \label{eq:ic}
\end{equation}
while the faradaic current follows the Cottrell equation \cite{bard2001},
\begin{equation}
  I_f(t) = \frac{nFA\sqrt{D}\,C^*}{\sqrt{\pi t}},
  \label{eq:cottrell}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of electrons transferred, $F$ is Faraday's constant,
$A$ is the electrode area, $D$ is the diffusion coefficient and $C^*$ is the
bulk analyte concentration. Because the exponential decays far faster than
$1/\sqrt{t}$, both samples are taken after the capacitive transient has died
away, so that $I_{c,\text{before}} \approx I_{c,\text{end}} \approx 0$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Delta I \approx I_{f,\text{end}} - I_{f,\text{before}}.
  \label{eq:delta_if}
\end{equation}
The full expansion is given in Appendix~\ref{app:dpv}. In practice this sets
a minimum pulse width: sampling at $t = 5R_sC_{dl}$ leaves less than
\SI{1}{\percent} of the initial capacitive current ($e^{-5} \approx 0.007$).
Because $\Delta I$ approximates the derivative of the faradaic
current--potential curve, the voltammogram forms a peak which, for a
reversible system, lies half a pulse amplitude away from the half-wave
potential $E_{1/2}$ \cite{bard2001}.

\section{Analog Front-End Stability}
\label{sec:afe_stability}

The AD5941 implements the three-amplifier topology of
Section~\ref{sec:3opamp_topology}: the potentiostat amplifier acts as the control
amplifier driving the CE, the RE is sensed through a high-impedance
unity-gain stage, and the high-speed TIA holds the WE at virtual ground and
converts the cell current to a voltage. The cell lies inside the feedback
loops of the control amplifier and the TIA, so its double-layer capacitance
affects the stability of both.

Each loop is assessed with the phase-margin criterion. The loop gain
$L(s) = A(s)\beta(s)$ is evaluated at the crossover frequency $\omega_c$,
where $|L(j\omega_c)| = 1$, and the phase margin
$\phi_\text{PM} = \SI{180}{\degree} + \angle L(j\omega_c)$ must be positive
for the loop to be stable, with \SIrange{45}{65}{\degree} giving a
well-damped response \cite{c2}. Both amplifiers are modeled with a single
dominant pole, $A(s) = \omega_t/s$ with $\omega_t = 2\pi \cdot
\text{GBW}$ \cite{bard2001}. The underlying feedback theory and the
derivations of the results below are given in Appendix~\ref{app:feedback}.

\subsection{Control Loop}
\label{sec:control_loop}

Only the high-frequency behaviour of the cell matters for stability. $R_{ct}$
and $Z_W$ govern the slow faradaic response and are omitted, which reduces
the cell to $C_{dl}$ in series with the uncompensated resistance $R_u$, the
part of $R_s$ between the RE and the WE \cite{bard2001, c7, c8}. This
reduced cell is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cell_buffer}.

The RE senses the voltage across $C_{dl}$, so the feedback factor is the
divider formed by $R_u$ and $C_{dl}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:beta}):
\begin{equation}
  \beta(s) = \frac{1}{1 + s\tau_\text{cell}},
  \qquad \tau_\text{cell} \equiv R_uC_{dl}.
  \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
The loop gain of the control amplifier is then
\begin{equation}
  L_\text{ctrl}(s) = \frac{\omega_{t,\text{ctrl}}}{s\,(1 + s\tau_\text{cell})},
  \label{eq:l_ctrl}
\end{equation}
which for $\omega \gg 1/\tau_\text{cell}$ crosses unity at
\begin{equation}
  \omega_{c,\text{ctrl}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{t,\text{ctrl}}}{\tau_\text{cell}}}.
  \label{eq:wc_ctrl}
\end{equation}
At this frequency the integrator contributes \SI{-90}{\degree} and the cell
pole adds lag approaching a further \SI{-90}{\degree} as
$\omega_{c,\text{ctrl}}\tau_\text{cell}$ grows, so the phase margin falls as
$\tau_\text{cell}$ increases. $R_u$ is typically tens to a few hundred ohms
in a well-conducting aqueous electrolyte \cite{bard2001}, so
$\tau_\text{cell}$ is much smaller than the $R_fC_{dl}$ time constant of the
TIA loop (Section~\ref{sec:tia_loop}), and the control loop is the less
critical of the two. Because the gain-bandwidth product of the control
amplifier is fixed inside the AD5941, this loop cannot be compensated in the
design; instead it limits the cells that can be driven, since a
high-resistance sample or a large electrode raises $\tau_\text{cell}$ and
erodes the margin. The RE buffer is a voltage follower with $\beta = 1$;
because its open-loop gain is large across the frequencies of interest, it
contributes no significant pole \cite{c3}.

\subsection{TIA Loop and Compensation}
\label{sec:tia_loop}

The WE connects directly to the inverting input of the TIA, so $C_{dl}$ shunts this node in the same way that
the junction capacitance of a photodiode shunts the input of a photodiode
amplifier \cite{graeme1996photodiode}.

With a feedback resistor $R_f$, $C_{dl}$ introduces a zero into the noise
gain (Appendix~\ref{app:tia}),
\begin{equation}
  NG(s) = 1 + sR_fC_{dl},
  \qquad f_z = \frac{1}{2\pi R_fC_{dl}},
  \label{eq:noise_gain}
\end{equation}
which appears as a second pole in the loop gain:
\begin{equation}
  L_\text{TIA}(s) = \frac{A(s)}{NG(s)}
    = \frac{\omega_t}{s\,(1 + sR_fC_{dl})}.
  \label{eq:l_tia}
\end{equation}
For $\omega \gg 1/R_fC_{dl}$ the crossover frequency is
\begin{equation}
  \omega_{c,\text{TIA}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\omega_t}{R_fC_{dl}}}.
  \label{eq:wc_tia}
\end{equation}
At this frequency both poles contribute close to \SI{-90}{\degree}, so the
phase margin collapses towards \SI{0}{\degree} and the TIA rings or
oscillates \cite{franco2014design}. Because $R_f$ must be large to resolve
nanoampere currents, $R_fC_{dl} \gg R_uC_{dl}$, which makes this the
critical loop.

The loop is stabilised by placing a capacitor $C_f$ in parallel with $R_f$.
$C_f$ adds a pole to the noise gain at $1/(2\pi R_fC_f)$, above the zero
caused by $C_{dl}$, which levels off the noise gain before it meets the
open-loop gain of the amplifier. Placing this pole at $f_z$ and the GBW gives a phase margin of about \SI{45}{\degree} or more
\cite{graeme1996photodiode, adi_mt059}, for which
\begin{equation}
  C_f = \sqrt{\frac{C_{dl}}{2\pi R_f \cdot \text{GBW}}}.
  \label{eq:cf}
\end{equation}
$C_f$ must therefore be sized for the largest expected $C_{dl}$ of the cell rather than chosen independently of it. In the AD5941, the capacitor in
parallel with the high-speed TIA gain resistor is programmable from
\SIrange{1}{63}{\pico\farad} in \SI{1}{\pico\farad} steps, alongside gain
resistors from \SI{200}{\ohm} to \SI{160}{\kilo\ohm}
\cite[p.~52]{ad5941_ds}, so Equation~\eqref{eq:cf} can be applied for each
gain range.
